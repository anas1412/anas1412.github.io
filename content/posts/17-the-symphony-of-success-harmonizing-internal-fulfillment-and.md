The psychologist Tal Ben-Shahar has a name for a particular disappointment:
**the arrival fallacy**. It is the belief that reaching a goal will make you
lastingly happy, followed by the discovery, on arrival, that it does not.

Most high achievers know the feeling. The promotion, the first big payout,
the finished project. A week of relief, and then a strange flatness, and then
the next goal, already chosen, already urgent.

## Two things that are often confused

| Achievement | Fulfilment |
|---|---|
| comes from outcomes | comes from how you live |
| is felt on arrival | is felt along the way |
| fades as you adapt to it | grows as you practise it |
| can be taken away | is much harder to lose |

The two failing models both chase achievement and hope fulfilment will come
bundled with it. It rarely does, because of the same hedonic adaptation
described in [[04-the-have-do-be-model-the-illusion-of-external-success|Have-Do-Be]]:
every arrival resets the baseline.

## How Be-Do-Have joins them

[[16-the-be-do-have-model-attracting-your-reality|Be-Do-Have]] does not ask
you to choose. It reorders them.

When the identity comes first and the actions express it, fulfilment is
available every day you act as that person, regardless of what the results
did that day. The achievements, when they arrive, stop being the point and
become evidence: confirmation that the way you are living works.

That creates a loop instead of a treadmill. Achievement reinforces identity.
Identity produces the actions. The actions produce more achievement, and none
of it has to carry the weight of making you happy.

## What changes

Setbacks stop being verdicts, because your sense of who you are was never
resting on the result. A losing week is a losing week, not a referendum on
your worth.

The destination still matters. It just stops being the only place you are
allowed to feel you have arrived. The rider and horse still have somewhere to
go, and they are no longer waiting until they get there to enjoy the ride.
