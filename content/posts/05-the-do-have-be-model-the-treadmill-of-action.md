*If I do enough, I will have enough, and then I can finally be okay.*

Do-Have-Be is what people graduate to when they realise that waiting for
permission never works. It deserves credit. Action beats waiting. Doing
builds skill, and skill compounds. Most of the celebrated careers you know
were built by people running this model hard.

It still has the same flaw in a different place.

## Three ways it breaks

**It locks you to outcomes.** The only thing that counts is the result, so
every day that fails to produce one registers as failure. Most days fail to
produce one. You spend most of your life feeling behind.

**Volume replaces direction.** When progress is measured in effort, more
effort always looks like the answer. More hours, more tasks, more meetings.
Nobody stops to ask whether the hours are pointed at anything.

**There is no rest state.** "Be" is still deferred to the end, so there is no
version of today where you are allowed to be enough. That is what burnout
is: a person running a model with no finish line.

## Busy is not progress

Traders call it overtrading. The trader who takes ten mediocre trades a day
because doing something feels productive is running Do-Have-Be on a
leveraged account. His edge was in the one good trade. The other nine were
the treadmill.

Engineering teams do it too: the team that is always firefighting and
therefore never has time to remove the cause of the fires.

The rider spurs the horse harder every morning. Neither of them knows the
destination. Eventually the horse goes lame and the rider blames the horse.

## The shared bug

Have-Do-Be and Do-Have-Be disagree about almost everything, except one thing
that matters most: **both put "be" last.** Both treat who you are as the
reward at the end instead of the starting point.

The series ends with the model that fixes that. First, the foundations it
stands on.
